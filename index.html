Objective 1 · section 14.1

Adding <ed>

The <ed> or <d> added to a regular verb is pronounced three different ways, and which one you get is decided entirely by the sound the verb already ends in — not by its spelling. Tap any word to hear it.

The only rule that adds a syllable is the first one. Needed has two syllables; walked and robbed still have one. If you add a syllable to walked, you have said a word that does not exist.

The old adjectives break the rule. A small group of very old words pronounce <ed> as a separate syllable /ɪd/ even though they are adjectives: naked, crooked, ragged, wretched, wicked, rugged, learned, cross-legged. So do adverbs ending in <edly>: supposedly, allegedly, markedly, deservedly, repeatedly. But a married woman and a borrowed book follow the normal rule.

Objective 2 · section 14.2

Adding <s>

The <s>, <es> or <'s> that makes a plural, a possessive or a third person singular works exactly like <ed>: voiceless with voiceless, voiced with voiced, and an extra syllable only when the word already ends in a hissing sound.

Test for the extra syllable: if the word already ends in /s, z, ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ/ you physically cannot add another /s/ or /z/ — your mouth needs a vowel in between. That is why judges sounds exactly like judge is.

Learn these by heart. These common words all take /z/ even though the spelling gives you no clue: is, was, has, does, goes, says, his, hers, theirs, yours, ours, always, sometimes. Contractions follow the same rules: it's /ɪts/, that's /ðæts/, she's /ʃiz/, there's /ðɛɚz/.

Objective 3 · section 14.3

Consonant groups

Adding an ending can leave four consonants in a row. English allows up to seven across two words — He glimpsed strangers is /mpststr/. Here are the five ways out, and the one thing you must never drop.

Never drop the ending itself. You may drop a middle consonant — acts /ækts/ becomes /æks/, months /mʌnθs/ becomes /mʌns/ — but the grammatical <ed> or <s> has to survive. Drop that and you have changed the grammar, not just the sound.
